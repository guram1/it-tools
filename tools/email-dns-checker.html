      What this checker does
      
            
      Mail receivers decide whether a message that claims to come from your domain is genuine by combining three DNS-published mechanisms. SPF (RFC 7208) lists the hosts allowed to use the domain in the SMTP envelope sender. DKIM (RFC 6376) publishes public keys that verify a cryptographic signature on the message. DMARC (RFC 7489, now RFC 9989) tells the receiver what to do when neither SPF nor DKIM passes and aligns with the visible From: domain, and where to send reports. This tool queries all of them, plus MX, over DNS-over-HTTPS and applies the limits that real receivers enforce, not just a syntax check.
      

            
      How the SPF evaluation works
      
            
      The checker fetches the TXT records at the domain and keeps only those that begin with exactly v=spf1 (so v=spf10 is discarded). Zero records means the SPF result is none; more than one is a permerror (RFC 7208 §4.5). Long records published as several 255-byte strings are joined without spaces, as §3.3 requires. It then walks the record the way a receiver does for a sender that matches nothing, which is the worst case:
      
            
        
                
      • Counted against the limit of 10: include, a, mx, ptr, exists and the redirect modifier. The counter is global: terms inside every nested include add to the same budget.
        • 
                
      • Not counted: ip4, ip6, all and exp.
        • 
                
      • Void lookups: a query that returns NXDOMAIN or an empty answer. RFC 7208 says implementations SHOULD stop at two; the third is a permerror. Typical causes are an include to a vendor domain that was shut down, or a: pointing to a decommissioned host.
        • 
                
      • Per-mechanism limits: each mx mechanism must not require more than 10 address lookups, so a domain with 11 MX hosts breaks mx by itself.
        • 
                
      • Targets without SPF: an include or redirect to a name with no SPF record is a permerror, not a silent pass-through.
        • 
                
      • Ordering rules: terms after all are never evaluated, and redirect is ignored when the record contains all.
        • 
            
      

            
      Worked example: github.com
      
            
      When we checked on 21 November 2026, github.com published v=spf1 ip4:192.30.252.0/22 include:spf.protection.outlook.com include:_netblocks.google.com include:_netblocks2.google.com include:mail.zendesk.com include:_spf.salesforce.com include:servers.mcsv.net include:mktomail.com include:sendgrid.net ip4:62.253.227.114 … ~all. That is 8 includes. Two of the included records contain one more include each (_spf.salesforce.com and sendgrid.net), so the total is 10 of 10. The record is valid today, but one extra include added by any of those vendors would push every message to permerror. The ip4 entries cost nothing. Compare google.com: v=spf1 include:_spf.google.com ~all uses one lookup, because _spf.google.com lists only ip4/ip6 ranges.
      

            
      Reading the DMARC result
      
            
      The tool queries _dmarc.<domain>. If there is no record it walks up the DNS tree (capped at eight queries, the RFC 9989 method) to find an inherited organizational policy. It then checks that v=DMARC1 is the first tag and that p is valid, parses sp, np, pct, adkim, aspf, fo, t, rua and ruf, and rates enforcement:
      
            
        
                
      • p=reject is full enforcement. p=quarantine sends failures to spam. p=none is monitoring only.
        • 
                
      • An sp weaker than p leaves existing subdomains open to spoofing even when the main domain is at reject.
        • 
                
      • Under RFC 7489, pct=25 applies the policy to a quarter of failing mail and treats the rest one level lower. RFC 9989 (Standards Track, May 2026) obsoletes RFC 7489, removes pct, and adds t=y as the testing signal, plus np for non-existent subdomains. Receivers are migrating, so the tool reports both readings.
        • 
                
      • A rua address in another organization's domain only receives reports if that domain publishes <yourdomain>._report._dmarc.<reportdomain> with v=DMARC1. The checker queries this record.
        • 
            
      

            
      DKIM selectors and key sizes
      
            
      Keys are published at selector._domainkey.domain. The checker tries your selectors plus google, selector1, selector2, k1, default, s1, s2, mail and dkim. It decodes the p= value and reads the RSA modulus length from the DER structure, or confirms a 32-byte Ed25519 key (RFC 8463). RFC 8301 requires RSA keys of at least 1024 bits and recommends 2048 or more. An empty p= means the key has been revoked. Vendor defaults differ. Google Workspace's default selector is google and offers 2048-bit or 1024-bit keys. Microsoft 365 uses the fixed host names selector1._domainkey and selector2._domainkey as CNAMEs into its own zone, and its New-DkimSigningConfig cmdlet defaults to a 1024-bit key unless you pass -KeySize 2048. A selector that is not found here does not prove DKIM is off, only that it is not one of the names tried.
      

            
      Common mistakes we see
      
            
        
                
      • Adding a second SPF record for a new vendor instead of merging it into the first. Both records become permerror.
        • 
                
      • Counting only includes. Google's setup page says an SPF record "can have up to 10 include: tags", but the RFC limit covers a, mx, exists, ptr and redirect too, recursively.
        • 
                
      • +all or ?all. +all authorizes the whole Internet. ?all returns neutral and protects nothing.
        • 
                
      • Leaving ptr in the record. RFC 7208 §5.5 says it SHOULD NOT be published: it is slow and depends on reverse DNS that the connecting party controls.
        • 
                
      • Staying on p=none for years, or publishing p=reject before reports confirm that every sender aligns.
        • 
                
      • Forgetting parked domains. A domain that sends no mail should publish v=spf1 -all, a DMARC p=reject, and a null MX (0 ., RFC 7505) if it also receives none.
        • 
            
      
            
      
                
      Frequently Asked Questions
      
                
      RFC 7208 section 4.6.4 caps the include, a, mx, ptr and exists mechanisms plus the redirect modifier at 10 per SPF evaluation, counted globally across every nested include. ip4, ip6, all and exp do not count. Above 10 the receiver returns permerror, which DMARC treats as an SPF failure. Remove unused includes, replace a and mx with ip4/ip6 where the addresses are stable, or move bulk senders to their own subdomain.
      
                
      No. RFC 7208 section 4.5 says that if more than one TXT record starting with v=spf1 exists at a name, the result is permerror. This usually happens when a second vendor's setup guide tells you to add a new record. Merge the mechanisms into one record with a single all at the end.
      
                
      DKIM keys live at selector._domainkey.domain, and the selector is chosen by the signer, so it cannot be listed from DNS. The tool tries common selectors such as google, selector1 and selector2. If yours is different, open a message you sent, read the s= value in the DKIM-Signature header, and enter it in the selector field.
      
                
      Start with p=none and an rua address to collect aggregate reports. Once the reports show that every legitimate sender passes SPF or DKIM with alignment, move to p=quarantine and then p=reject. p=none on its own does not stop spoofing; it only gives you visibility.
      
                
      The page sends DNS questions directly from your browser to Cloudflare's public DNS-over-HTTPS resolver at cloudflare-dns.com, and only when you press Check or open a link that contains a domain. OPS-BOX does not receive or store the domain. The record validator and the generators run entirely in your browser and make no network requests.
